Why PLAAFP Matters

Before we can write meaningful goals, we need to truly understand where a student is right now — academically, functionally, and as a whole person.

Section 01 · Why PLAAFP Matters

What Is a PLAAFP Statement?

The Present Levels of Academic Achievement and Functional Performance (PLAAFP) statement summarizes where a student is right now. It is the foundation of every IEP — without it, nothing else holds.

More Than a Score Summary

A strong PLAAFP is not a list of test scores. It tells the story of a student — what they can do, what challenges them, and what they need to move forward. Think of Marcus, a 4th grader who reads at a 2nd grade level but demonstrates remarkable verbal reasoning. His PLAAFP should capture both.

Section 01 · Why PLAAFP Matters

The Legal Foundation

The PLAAFP requirement is not just best practice — it is the law. Courts have been clear about what happens when it's missing.

If the IEP fails to assess the child's present levels of academic achievement and functional performance, the IEP does not comply with IDEA. This deficiency goes to the heart of the IEP; the child's level of academic achievement and functional performance is the foundation on which the IEP must be built. Without a clear identification of present levels, the IEP cannot set measurable goals, evaluate the child's progress, or determine which educational and related services are needed.

— Kirby v. Cabell County Board of Education, 2006

Element 3 Deep Dive: Baseline Data

Baseline data is the most commonly underdeveloped element. Compare these examples to see the difference meaningful data makes.

⚠ Weak Baseline✅ Strong Baseline
"Marcus has difficulty reading.""Marcus reads 58 words correct per minute on 2nd grade passages (Aimsweb, Sept. 2024), compared to a benchmark of 90 wcpm."
"Marcus struggles with math.""Marcus scores 62% on multi-step word problems involving addition and subtraction (district CFA, Oct. 2024). He solves single-step problems with 85% accuracy."
"Marcus has behavior challenges in class.""Marcus engages in off-task behavior an average of 4 times per 30-minute class period (direct observation, Oct. 2024), most frequently during independent work."
"Marcus's writing needs work.""Marcus produces an average of 12 correct writing sequences per 3-minute probe (AIMS, Sept. 2024), compared to a peer median of 24."
Key TakeawayStrong baseline data answers: What was measured? What were the results? When? Compared to what standard or benchmark?
03

Common Pitfalls

Even experienced case managers fall into predictable PLAAFP traps. Knowing them is the first step to avoiding them.

Section 03 · Common Pitfalls

Four Pitfalls — and How to Fix Them

👆 Click each card to reveal the fix

🚫 Pitfall 1: The Disability Label as Description
"Marcus has dyslexia, which causes reading difficulties." The label replaces actual description of the student's current performance.
✅ The Fix:Describe what the student actually does and doesn't do — not what their diagnosis says they should do. Use data and observation, not category language.
🚫 Pitfall 2: Only Deficits, No Strengths
A PLAAFP that reads like a list of everything the student can't do. It feels deficit-focused and doesn't give the team anything to build on.
✅ The Fix:Intentionally lead with or weave in documented strengths. For Marcus: "demonstrates strong verbal comprehension and can retell stories accurately when read aloud."
🚫 Pitfall 3: Vague or Stale Data
"Based on last year's evaluation…" or "According to teacher reports, Marcus sometimes struggles." Vague language and outdated data don't support goal-setting.
✅ The Fix:Cite specific tools, scores, dates, and observers. If data is more than a year old, note it and supplement with recent classroom data or informal assessments.
🚫 Pitfall 4: No Connection to Goals
The PLAAFP and the annual goals feel like they came from two different documents. A reader can't trace how one led to the other.
✅ The Fix:After drafting each PLAAFP element, ask: "Which goal does this connect to?" If you can't answer, either the PLAAFP needs more specificity or a goal is missing.

IEP Team Guiding Questions

Use these questions to drive your team's PLAAFP conversation. Click each to mark it as discussed.

What can this student do well — academically, functionally, and socially?
What specific skills or behaviors are areas of need, and what data supports this?
How does the student's disability affect their access to and progress in the general education curriculum?
What does current assessment data tell us? Is it recent enough to be meaningful?
What is the student's starting point — what baseline will we measure progress against?
What accommodations or supports are already working for this student?
What does the parent/guardian observe at home that connects to school performance?
For students old enough: What does the student say about how they learn best?

💡 These questions help ensure your PLAAFP captures the whole student — not just the disability.

Functional Performance Examples

PLAAFP statements address more than academics. Explore examples across three functional domains — click a tab to explore.

Weak Example

"Marcus has trouble making friends and sometimes gets upset."

Strong Example

"Marcus initiates peer interactions in unstructured settings (lunch, recess) an average of 1–2 times per day. When peer interactions do not go as expected, Marcus has exited the situation without adult support on 6 of 10 observed occasions (Oct. 2024). He has identified two trusted adults he can go to when overwhelmed."

Weak Example

"Marcus has behavior issues that affect his learning."

Strong Example

"During independent work periods, Marcus engages in off-task behaviors (pencil tapping, looking around) an average of 4 times per 30-minute period, with each episode lasting approximately 2–3 minutes (direct observation, Oct. 2024). Off-task behavior occurs least frequently during small-group activities."

Weak Example

"Marcus has fine motor difficulties."

Strong Example

"Marcus produces legible handwriting at a rate of approximately 8 letters per minute on copying tasks (OT assessment, Sept. 2024). The grade-level expectation is 15+ letters per minute. He demonstrates pencil grip adjustments when fatigued, which affects sustained written output in longer tasks."

Context

District & State Format Variation

While the four required elements are consistent nationwide, how they're documented varies. Here are two common formats.

📄 Format A: Unified Narrative

  • One comprehensive statement covering all areas of need
  • Strengths and challenges woven together
  • All four elements integrated into flowing prose
  • Common in states with flexible IEP formats
  • Best for: Experienced writers who can weave all elements naturally

📋 Format B: Area-by-Area Sections

  • Separate PLAAFP entry for each area of need (e.g., Reading, Math, Behavior)
  • Each section addresses all four elements for that domain
  • Easier to audit for compliance
  • Common in states with structured IEP templates
  • Best for: Teams new to PLAAFP writing or complex multi-domain students

What Stays Consistent

Regardless of format, all four required elements must be present for every identified area of need. Format affects organization — not what must be included. When in doubt, check your state's IEP guidance documents or consult your district's special education coordinator.
